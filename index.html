Q10 calculator by condition

Paste three columns (temperature, period, condition) to fit the temperature dependence (Q10) of the circadian period for each condition and compare them. Everything is calculated in your browser; your data is not sent anywhere.

Data

One row per measurement: temperature (°C), period (h), condition. Paste from Excel (tab-delimited) or use CSV. Rows whose period is blank or not a number are treated as having no data at that temperature. A header line and lines starting with # are skipped.

Base temperature

The temperature at which τb is reported, shared by all conditions. Q10 and R² do not depend on it.

        How this calculator works
        
    Model
    
        
    For each condition, the period at the base temperature (τb) and Q10 are estimated by non-linear least squares on the period itself (Levenberg–Marquardt), as in the single-condition calculator.
    
        
    τ(T) = τb · Q10(Tb − T)/10
    
        
    Q10 > 1: the period shortens as temperature rises. Q10 = 1: perfect temperature compensation. Q10 < 1: overcompensation. Starting values come from a straight-line regression of ln(period) on temperature.
    
        
    Errors and confidence intervals
    
        
    Standard errors come from the diagonal of s²·(JᵀJ)⁻¹ with s² = SSE/(n − 2). The 95% CI of Q10 is built on the log scale from the SE of ln Q10 and the t value with n − 2 degrees of freedom, so it is asymmetric. Conditions with fewer than three points get no errors.
    
        
    Comparison between conditions
    
        
    Overall test. An extra sum-of-squares F test compares a model with a separate τb per condition but one shared Q10 against a model with a separate Q10 per condition: F(k − 1, N − 2k). A small p suggests that Q10 differs in at least one condition. It assumes similar residual scatter in all conditions.
    
        
    Pairwise comparisons. The difference in ln Q10 is tested with t = Δ / √(SE₁² + SE₂²) on n₁ + n₂ − 4 degrees of freedom, and p values are adjusted by Holm’s method. This is an approximation; treat it as a guide.
    
        
    Arrhenius
    
        
    Frequency is 24/period (day⁻¹). The Arrhenius plot shows ln(frequency) against 1000/T (K). “Ea (from Q10)” is Ea ≈ R·T₁·T₂·ln Q10 / 10, with T₁ and T₂ the lowest and highest fitted temperatures in kelvin. “Ea (regression)” comes from the slope of a straight-line regression of ln(frequency) on 1000/T.
    
        
    Polynomial fit
    
        
    Optionally, a polynomial of period on temperature (degree 1–6) is fitted to each condition by ordinary least squares, using all of that condition’s data or only the data between its own Low and High. It does not affect the Q10 fit. The curve is drawn over the condition’s full temperature span, extended by 10% on each side (outside the fitted data this is an extrapolation). The report lists the equation, coefficients with standard errors, R², adjusted R² and the residual standard error; ticking “Include fitted curves” in the Report settings adds 200 calculated points of each Q10 and polynomial curve per condition. A fit needs at least degree + 1 distinct temperatures, and standard errors need more points than coefficients.
    
        
    Input format
    
        
    Tab-delimited input is recommended (paste three columns straight from Excel). Comma and semicolon delimiters also work; quote condition names that contain commas. Avoid space-delimited input, because a blank period cannot be told apart from the condition name. Rows with an empty condition are grouped as “(no label)”.
    
        
    Reference
    
        
    Based on the single-condition Q10 calculator for circadian period, applying the same fit to each condition.
